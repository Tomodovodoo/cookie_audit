What we test.
What the results mean.

This project screens public website homepages for observable cookie consent problems. It is independent research, not a legal assessment by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Findings concern the captured visit, browser, location and date.

Which websites are in scope?

Dutch cookie rules concern storing information on, or accessing information from, a user's device. They cover similar technologies as well as cookies. The requirement is not limited to companies with a .nl address. Consent is generally required for tracking cookies; strictly necessary uses and analytics with no or limited privacy impact can be exempt. A website that only uses exempt technologies need not show an accept/reject banner.

Where personal data is processed, the GDPR applies to processing in the context of an EU establishment, and in specified circumstances to organisations outside the EU that offer goods or services to people in the EU or monitor their behaviour there. A site's presence in a popularity ranking alone does not establish jurisdiction or the AP's lead supervisory role. We do not automatically adjudicate legal territorial scope for each domain.

Sources: AP banner guidance, ACM cookie exemptions, AP cookies and Article 11.7a, GDPR Articles 3, 4 and 7. Checked 3 November 2026.

The refusal standard

The AP expects acceptance and refusal on the same layer. Refusal must not take extra clicks or unnecessary scrolling. A refusal option must be visible and readable, not concealed as an inconspicuous link in text. A different button colour alone does not establish a violation. Our numerical size and contrast thresholds are screening heuristics, not thresholds published by the AP.

How each visit works

  1. Open the homepage in a fresh, isolated Chrome context with Dutch language, Amsterdam timezone and a 1365 × 900 viewport. No personal browser profile is used. The current machine's egress was checked as Netherlands through ipapi.co; language settings alone cannot establish location.
  2. Observe for seven seconds after the initial document loads. Record visible text, controls and computed geometry across accessible frames and open shadow roots, a screenshot, cookie metadata, storage key names, and redacted request destinations.
  3. Attempt an explicit refusal. If needed, open settings and look for an explicit reject option, with a maximum of four clicks. We do not assume that closing a window or saving unknown defaults is refusal.
  4. Observe after refusal, reload and observe again. Check whether the choice interface returns and whether known advertising identifiers appear.
  5. When acceptance was detected, run an isolated acceptance control in a separate fresh context. This helps reviewers compare the site's behaviour. It does not automatically turn ambiguous analytics into a failure.

Chrome runs offscreen. The launcher omits its enable-automation flag and disables the AutomationControlled Blink feature. This reduces one automation signal; it does not make Chrome undetectable. Challenges and blocks remain inconclusive. The crawler never uses the person's logged-in browser, logs into sites, buys anything, or bypasses access controls.

The five checks

  • Cookie reject. Green means a direct refusal completed and its choice interface disappeared. Red means an acceptance option was found but refusal was absent on that layer, required scrolling or took more clicks. Grey means detection or completion was uncertain.
  • Equal prominence. Green means the controls passed the configured visibility and styling tests. Red flags a missing refusal option, extra scrolling, an unboxed text-sized refusal control beside a full acceptance button, low measured contrast, or substantially smaller text. Closed shadow styling, unusual backgrounds and complex layouts can defeat these heuristics.
  • No tracking before consent. Red means a known advertising identifier cookie was present before any consent action. Grey covers analytics cookies or requests to tracker-listed endpoints whose actual purpose needs review. Green means no configured signals were observed in that window.
  • No tracking after refusal. Uses the same rules after a completed refusal and again after reload. Grey also means no refusal journey completed. A pre-existing advertising identifier remaining after refusal is flagged even if no later transmission is proven.
  • Refusal persists. Green means the choice interface did not return after reload. This is a UI persistence observation, not proof that every vendor respected the choice. Red means the interface returned.

Tracker lists are clues, not legal verdicts. Loading a library, a consent-mode ping, or contacting a third-party host is not automatically treated as unlawful tracking. Functional cookies are not automatically failures. The code deliberately distinguishes advertising identifiers from analytics identifiers that may have exempt configurations.

Coverage and accuracy limits

The source is the NetAPI Netherlands top 1,000, downloaded on 3 November 2026. It combines country-level CrUX popularity groups with other ranking signals; its exact order is not a measured traffic league table. We retained the original ranks. A StevenBlack adult-domain list and explicit exclusions remove 124 entries, leaving 876. To reach 1,000 targets, we add the 124 highest-ranked remaining eligible .nl domains from the NetAPI global ranking. These additions are labelled supplemental and retain their global ranks. There is no claim that this combined cohort is exactly the top 1,000 non-adult sites visited from the Netherlands. Mixed-content platforms may remain; the filter is not a page-content classifier. See exclusions and source timestamps and hashes.

This is a first implementation with calibration on the user-named examples, not a measured accuracy benchmark. It does not detect all fingerprinting, first-party proxy tracking, server-side data sharing, IndexedDB use, service-worker traffic, transient cookies, or tracking triggered by later navigation. It tests one desktop viewport and short windows. Geo variation, slow banners, A/B tests, anti-bot pages, consent carried through redirects and inaccessible frames can change results. A disappearing banner alone cannot prove that optional purposes were actually disabled. The table must not be used to claim complete GDPR compliance or confirmed legal violations.

New captures also read available Cookiebot and IAB TCF consent receipts without retaining consent strings or user IDs. Advertising or special-feature consent still granted after refusal is flagged. Earlier captures without a consent_state field did not receive this additional check. The method draws on CookieAudit, CookieEnforcer, and ConsentCrawl; their published accuracy figures do not apply to this implementation.

Evidence and sorting

Each attempted row links to its JSON result and initial capture. Later stage screenshots and JSON files are in the matching repository evidence directory. SHA-256 hashes identify the published files. Request query strings, bodies, cookie values, storage values and authentication headers are omitted. Failed or incomplete visits retain uncertainty; untested sites are never filled with guessed results.

Default sorting counts failed checks per site, then original popularity rank. Company grouping uses documented brand lists, and orders groups by total failed checks. This favours larger groups and is not a measure of legal severity. Ownership not verified from a source is labelled as such; the domain remains the site identifier.

Privacy and use

This report has no analytics scripts, advertising pixels, remote fonts or optional cookies. GitHub Pages supplies hosting and may process access logs under GitHub's privacy statement. Website screenshots remain subject to their owners' rights and are included as evidence.
